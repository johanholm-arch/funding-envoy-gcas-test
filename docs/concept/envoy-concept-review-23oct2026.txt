
Envoy Concept Review

  Concept review · Agentic grant submission assistant · 23 Oct 2026
  Envoy: a requestor-side agent for grant requests

  An assessment of the premise, what I would add to it, and a clickable walkthrough of the end-to-end flow. "Envoy" is a working name. The example throughout is an accredited independent medical education grant from a US medical society, because it has the eligibility rules, documents, attestations, dates, reconciliation and outcomes reporting that stress every part of the design. The company this is built for first is not named anywhere in this document or the prototype; everything is configurable per company.

  In briefWhat Envoy does

    Envoy sits in front of a company's grants management system and talks to the people asking for funding. A requestor describes what they need in their own words, uploads what they have, or fills in a form. Envoy works out which kind of request it is, what the company's policy requires for that kind of request in that country, asks for what is missing, checks what is there, and writes a complete, review-ready request into the system of record when the requestor says so. Afterwards it handles the follow-ups: the coordinator's questions, the reconciliation, the outcomes report. People make every funding decision.

    GuidesExplains eligibility, grant types, funding rules and country requirements in plain language, grounded in the company's own policy documents and citing them. Declines to predict or promise a funding decision.

    Takes any inputConversation, uploaded documents (request letter, prospectus, agenda, budget, proof of non-profit status, tax forms, financial statements) or a form. Everything extracted shows where it came from.

    Checks as it goesRuns the company's rulebook continuously: required fields and documents per grant type and country, the revenue concentration cap, lead time before the activity, conflict-of-interest questions, attestations. Each finding says what to do and why.

    Submits on confirmationMatches the organization to an existing record, creates the request, attaches documents and a pre-submission review summary, and submits. Nothing is written to the system of record until the requestor confirms, and the requestor's own identity is on the record.

    Follows upTurns "the coordinator needs more information" into a short, focused exchange; collects reconciliation data and outcomes when they fall due; explains status in words a requestor understands.

    ConfigurableEach company is a tenant: logo, colour, type, terminology, languages, grant catalogue, rulebook, policy documents and connector settings. The layout and the safety rules stay fixed.

    External requestorSubmit once, completely
Medical societies, hospitals, universities, patient groups and charities. Most submit a handful of times a year and never learn the portal. They get a guide instead of a form.

    Grant coordinatorsReview, don't chase
Requests arrive with the checklist already satisfied, evidence attached and open points flagged. Fewer "request additional information" rounds, each one shorter.

    Compliance and medicalOne definition of compliant
The rulebook the agent enforces is the same one the platform's checklist enforces. Policy changes propagate to both. Every agent action is logged on the request.

    For the vendorA product, not a project
A requestor-side companion to the existing coordinator-side review agent, sold per company, configured without code, and connected through one adapter to the Salesforce-based grants platform.

  What it is not: a decision engine for funding, a replacement for the grants portal's forms (they remain the fallback and the control), or a system that trains models on requestor data.

    Feasible

      Buildable with today's models, if two rules hold

      Every capability in the vision statement can be built now. The first rule that makes it work in a regulated setting: the agent prepares, a deterministic rulebook validates, the system of record decides. The model reads a prospectus and drafts the fields, but the rulebook, not the model, says whether the request is complete and compliant. The model explains a policy clause, but it quotes the clause. The agent writes to the grants system, but only at confirmed checkpoints, as the requestor, through the platform's own validation.

      The second rule: the agent never touches a funding decision. It does not predict approval, negotiate amounts, or write the scientific rationale for the requestor. It makes the request complete and honest; the committee makes it approved or not. Requestors will try to use it as a coach for getting to yes. The design has to make that line visible.

      The hard parts are not the AI. They are: writing cleanly into a packaged Salesforce application under the requestor's identity; keeping one rulebook that the agent, the portal's checklist and the vendor's coordinator-side review agent all read; the sprawl of grant subtypes, triage groups and country rules; and deciding how much review moves forward to submission time without weakening the review that happens after.

  Context the agent has to carryThe grants landscape

    "Grant" covers several different things with different rules. A pharmaceutical company's grants function typically handles three families, each with its own eligibility, documents, reviewers and reporting. A fourth, investigator-initiated research, usually runs in a separate process and is out of scope here.

    Independent medical education

      Accredited: the provider is accredited (for example by ACCME in the US) and controls content, faculty and learners. The company may fund; it may not influence. A written agreement, disclosure to learners, and outcomes reporting are standard.
Non-accredited: education without credit. Same independence principle, but no accreditor stands behind it, so the company's own review of the programme, provider and budget is heavier.

      Example in the mockups: a 2-day heart-failure symposium run by a medical society with an accredited joint provider. US audience, so it routes to the US medical education reviewers.

    Other grants and donations

      Academic support, scientific congresses and meetings, scientific programmes and projects
Patient education and advocacy
Corporate memberships of associations
Donations of products, equipment or material; humanitarian product donations
Public policy and legislative support; independent patient assistance (US only)

      Eligibility turns on the organization type (association, hospital, university, patient group, trade association, business coalition) as much as on the activity. Several subtypes exist only for some countries.

    Community and charitable contributions

      Charitable donations to registered non-profits
Humanitarian relief, global health programmes
Company-run award schemes, STEM and community programmes

      Lighter on scientific review, heavier on the charity's status, governance, sanctions and anti-bribery screening, and on the "no business benefit" test. Reconciliation often reduces to a financial statement; some countries forbid asking for a post-activity report on a pure donation.

  The rules that bind a request
Most of what the agent enforces comes from one of four layers. The company's rulebook (next section) records which layer each rule comes from, so the requestor can be told why.

    Layer
Where the rule comes from
What it means for a request

    Law and enforcement
US anti-kickback statute and the OIG's 2003 compliance programme guidance; the Sunshine Act and Open Payments reporting; state laws; the FCPA and UK Bribery Act for government-affiliated institutions abroad; sanctions and debarment lists; local laws such as France's anti-gift regime that require declared conventions.
Grants must be separate from sales, awarded on objective criteria, not tied to prescribing, and documented. The company must know who is being paid and whether a payment is reportable. Government-affiliated recipients need anti-bribery due diligence.

    Industry codes
PhRMA Code and AdvaMed Code in the US; the EFPIA Code in Europe and its national implementations (ABPI, FSA and others); accreditation standards such as ACCME's Standards for Integrity and Independence.
Education grants go to organizations, never to individual professionals; the provider controls content, faculty and learners; the company's support is disclosed; in Europe, grants and donations to healthcare organizations are published yearly, by recipient.

    Company policy and SOP
The company's grants and donations policy and its operating procedure: eligible organizations, grant subtypes, triage to reviewing groups by grant type and audience geography, required documents, committee approval, agreement before the activity, payment by milestone, reconciliation and outcomes.
This is most of the rulebook. Typical rules: a cap on the share of a recipient's annual revenue the company may fund in a year; approval and an executed agreement before the activity starts; a minimum lead time; conflict-of-interest and anti-discrimination attestations; third-party risk screening above a threshold.

    Reviewing group and country practice
Each triage group (US medical, US public affairs, US and UK charitable, global health, global committee, local affiliates) and each country adds required fields, documents and exceptions.
The same request needs different things in different places. The rulebook scopes every rule by grant type, organization type, country and reviewing group, and the agent only asks for what applies.

  Where this document names specific thresholds, lead times or documents, they are illustrative of the kind of rule a company sets, not a statement of any one company's policy.

    Natural-language intake ("I'd like to request $50,000 for a CME programme in Boston")
High
Extraction into a fixed schema per grant subtype. The agent asks for what the schema still needs, in an order that makes sense to the requestor, not in the order of the form.
Requestors describe activities, not grant subtypes. Classification into the company's catalogue (accredited vs non-accredited, education vs congress support) has to be confirmed with the requestor, with the consequences explained, before the form shape is fixed.

    Document upload and extraction
High
Prospectuses, agendas, budgets, request letters, tax forms and financial statements are well structured. Every extracted value carries its source (file, page, cell) and the requestor confirms before anything is saved.
Budgets rarely add up to the amount requested; dates differ between agenda and letter. The agent must surface conflicts, not pick one. Scanned forms need OCR. Bank details should never pass through the conversation.

    Dynamic compliance review
High
A deterministic rulebook scoped by grant type, organization type, country and reviewing group. Checks run on every change. Each finding has severity, an action and a basis.
The rulebook must stay identical to the platform's checklist rules or requests will pass the agent and fail on submit. Treat the checklist as the source and import it; add agent-only rules on top.

    Policy guidance ("Am I eligible?", "Which grant type?", country rules)
High, with limits
Retrieval over the company's policy, SOP and public grants pages, with the clause quoted. Eligibility is answered by running the rulebook on what is known so far, not by the model's opinion.
Questions outside the documents ("will this be approved?", "how much do you usually give?") must be declined and routed to the coordinator mailbox. Multilingual guidance needs the policy in each language or careful translation review.

    Writing structured requests to the Salesforce grants platform
Medium
Standard REST and composite APIs, Files upload, and the platform's own validation on save. Writes happen at a few checkpoints (organization matched, request created, documents attached, submitted) and are idempotent.
Identity and audit: the request should be created by the requestor, not a service account. Organizations already exist twice (entity and payee), so matching beats creating. The package's custom objects, picklists and checklist rules must be read from the org, not hard-coded. See the Salesforce section.

    Ongoing validation and follow-ups (additional information, reconciliation, outcomes)
High
Status changes and coordinator comments arrive as events. The agent shows the requestor only what is being asked, collects it, writes it, and leaves a change summary for the coordinator.
Today's "request additional information" step reopens every field for the requestor and coordinators struggle to see what changed. The agent's change summary fixes that, but only if the coordinator reads it in the platform, which means writing it as a comment or document there.

    Alternative input form
High
The same schema rendered as a form, section by section, with the same checks. The portal's existing forms remain the fallback and the control.
Two forms for one request (agent's and portal's) confuse requestors if both are visible. Decide where the agent lives (inside the portal or beside it) early.

    Configurable to any company
High
Tenant configuration: brand tokens, terminology, languages, grant catalogue, rulebook, policy corpus, connector mapping. No code per company.
Terminology and grant catalogues differ more than branding does. The catalogue and the rulebook need an import path from each company's platform configuration, otherwise every onboarding is a consulting project.

    Agent autonomy
Medium, by design
The agent drafts and checks on its own; it writes only on confirmation; it never submits, attests, signs or accepts terms for the requestor.
Pressure to "just submit it for me". The attestations (conflict of interest, anti-discrimination, scope and transfer-of-value at reconciliation) are the requestor's legal statements and must be typed or ticked by them, visibly.

  A note on validation: a grants process is not a GxP process, so formal computer system validation is rarely mandated. The platform it writes to is validated and audited, though, and the audit trail there must be able to show which values the agent proposed, which the requestor confirmed and which were later changed. Building that provenance in from day one costs little and answers the first question an auditor will ask.

  Before and after submissionWhere compliance review moves

    The brief asks the agent to take much of the completeness and compliance review off the platform, and also flags the need to balance review before and after submission. My view: move the checking forward, keep the judging where it is. The coordinator stops chasing missing documents and arithmetic and spends the review on the things that need a person: is this a legitimate educational need, is the provider independent, is the budget reasonable, is there a business benefit hiding in a charitable ask.

  The practical consequence: a submission carries a pre-submission review summary (checks run, evidence, open points, what the requestor changed) as a document on the request. The coordinator's first look starts from that summary instead of from the raw form.

  Where the policy livesRulebook and grant catalogue

    The rulebook is the configuration that makes the agent a product rather than a prompt. It is a list of checks, each scoped and sourced, that the agent runs deterministically. The grant catalogue describes each subtype: its fields, documents, reviewing group and the questions the agent asks to tell it apart from its neighbours. Screen 2 in the mockups shows both.

    A rule hasAn identifier; a plain-language statement; its basis (law, code, company policy clause, reviewing-group practice); its scope (grant subtypes, organization types, countries, reviewing groups); a check type (field present, document present, computed, date logic, cross-reference, attestation); a severity (block, warn, inform); the message to the requestor in each language; and the action offered.

    Imported from the platformThe platform's checklist rules per reviewing group are the source of truth for completeness. They are imported, not retyped, and a nightly comparison flags drift. A request that passes the agent and fails the platform's validation is a defect, logged and fixed in the rulebook.

    Added in the agentChecks the platform cannot do at submission time because it does not yet have the data: budget arithmetic against the request, lead time against the agenda, independence clauses in a prospectus, document classification, cross-document consistency.

    Owned and versionedA content owner per company (usually the grants process owner) approves rulebook changes. Published versions never change; every submission records the rulebook version it was checked against. A policy revision produces a diff and a list of rules affected.

    1ImportGrant catalogue, picklists and checklist rules read from the company's platform configuration.

    2EnrichThe vendor's grants practice adds the agent-only checks and the plain-language messages, sourced to the policy.

    3TestSample requests per subtype and country run against the rulebook and against the platform sandbox; results must agree.

    4PublishProcess owner signs off a version. The agent and, where adopted, the coordinator-side review agent read it.

    5MonitorSubmissions rejected or returned for information are analysed against the rulebook. Frequent gaps become new checks or clearer questions.

  This is also where the vendor's advantage sits. The software is the same for every company. The catalogue of checks, the questions that tell subtypes apart, and the plain-language explanations of policy are the practice's accumulated knowledge, and they transfer between clients with the company-specific parts stripped out.

  IntegrationConnecting to the Salesforce grants platform

    The grants system is a packaged application on the Salesforce platform with an internal coordinator app and an external requestor portal. Requestors register an organization profile, submit requests and respond to information requests, reconciliation and outcomes through the portal. The connector has to respect how that application already works. The points below come from the coordinator manual and from how packaged Salesforce applications behave.

    Consideration
What the platform does today
What the connector must do

    Identity and audit
Requests are created by the registered requestor user. Automatic emails go to the requestor registered at creation; changing the requestor afterwards is a backend ticket. Field history and approval history are kept on every request.
Act as the requestor: the portal's login grants the agent a user-scoped token, so created-by and the history are the requestor's. A service account with "submitted on behalf of" fields is the fallback, not the design. Every agent write adds a history entry or comment that names the agent.

    Organizations exist twice
An organization is an entity record (profile, identifiers, revenue, documents) and a payee record (bank details). Identifiers such as tax IDs are how duplicates are found.
Search before create, by identifier and name. Never create a second entity for a known organization; never collect bank details in the conversation. Payee information stays in the portal's own secure flow, which the agent links to.

    Packaged objects and rules
Custom objects for request, activities, audience breakdown, documents with category and type, comments visible to portal users, checklist rules per reviewing group, automatic triage by grant type and audience geography.
Read object metadata and picklists from the org at configuration time, not from a hard-coded map. Write through the platform's validation; surface its errors to the requestor in plain language. Classify documents into the platform's categories.

    Checkpointed writes
Status moves from new to submitted for review, then through approval, agreement, payment, reconciliation and closure. Only the requestor's submit moves a request out of draft.
Write at four checkpoints: organization matched or created, request created in draft, documents attached, submit. Each write is idempotent with a correlation key held in the working request, so a retry never duplicates.

    Events from the platform
Coordinators request additional information (an email with no detail; the comment is in the portal), trigger reconciliation (automatically 90 days after the activity ends, or manually), and request outcomes for education grants.
Subscribe to status and comment changes (platform events or change data capture; polling as fallback). Turn each into a focused task for the requestor with the coordinator's comment quoted, and write back a change summary.

    Where the agent runs
The vendor offers an agent platform; Salesforce offers Agentforce inside the org.
Keep the agent a separate service with a Salesforce adapter. It makes the product configurable across companies and platforms, lets each company choose model hosting and residency, and keeps the grants org's licensing simple. Embed it in the portal as a component where the company wants one login and one place.

    Environments and limits
Sandboxes per company; API and storage limits; seven portal languages.
Build and certify against each company's sandbox; respect limits with batched composite calls; test the four checkpoints end to end before go-live (screen 3).

  RecommendationsMissing functionality

    Beyond the five requirements in the discussion deck, grouped by how soon you need them. "Must" items are ones I'd expect a grants or compliance lead to ask about in the first demo.

    MustField-level provenance and a working request
Every value shows where it came from (message, document page, profile) and whether the requestor confirmed it. Drafts are resumable across sessions. Without this the agent is a chat window, not a submission tool. Screens 5 and 6.

    MustOne rulebook, imported from the platform
The checklist rules per reviewing group are the source. The agent imports them, adds its own, and a test set proves they agree. A request that passes the agent and fails on submit is the fastest way to lose requestors. Screen 2.

    MustRequestor identity and attestations
Writes happen under the requestor's portal login. Conflict-of-interest answers, anti-discrimination and independence statements are typed or ticked by the requestor, visibly, and recorded as theirs. Screen 8.

    MustAgent activity written to the request
Every extraction, proposal, change and attachment appears in the platform's history and as a review summary document. Auditors and coordinators see it where they already look. Screen 10.

    MustScope boundaries
Declines to predict decisions, suggest amounts, or write the educational rationale. Explains why, offers the coordinator contact. The line between completeness coaching and advocacy must be enforced in the orchestrator, not left to the prompt.

    MustOrganization matching
Search by identifier and name before creating; reuse profile documents (non-profit letter, W-9, financial statement) and tell the requestor when they are stale. Duplicate organizations are a backend ticket to fix today.

    ShouldRequired-actions inbox
Additional information requests, reconciliation, outcomes and signature reminders in one list, each turned into a short exchange with the coordinator's comment quoted and a change summary written back. Screen 9.

    ShouldDocument intelligence beyond extraction
Budget arithmetic, ineligible lines, date consistency across letter, agenda and budget, independence clauses in a prospectus, and classification into the platform's document categories.

    ShouldStatus in plain language with expectations
"Being reviewed, a coordinator claimed it on 3 Nov, typical decision 6 to 8 weeks" instead of a status code. Expectations come from the company's own history, not from the model.

    ShouldTenant studio with import
Brand tokens, terminology, languages, catalogue, rulebook and connector mapping configured by the vendor's team without code, with the catalogue and picklists read from each company's org. Screens 1 to 3.

    ShouldInternal organizer mode
Coordinators who create requests on behalf of organizations (memberships, product donations, humanitarian relief) face the same completeness work and can use the same agent with internal identity.

    ShouldBlocker analytics
Which rules block most, which questions requestors abandon on, which triage predictions miss. Feeds the rulebook and the company's own policy clarity.

    LaterEmail intake and reminders
A request letter emailed to the grants mailbox starts a draft and invites the requestor into the conversation. Reconciliation reminders go out from the agent with the list of what is needed.

    LaterModel governance pack
Evaluation sets per grant subtype and language, a record of which model version did what, change control on model upgrades, and a statement on data use for each company's AI governance committee.

    LaterSecond platform adapter
The connector is an adapter. A second grants platform proves the agent is a product rather than an extension of one system, and sets the price of the first adapter.

    LaterEnterprise baseline
Single sign-on for the vendor's studio, encryption, data residency options per company, retention aligned to the company's records policy, and a security report. A gate for procurement, not a feature.

  What could go wrongRisks and mitigations

    The agent gives wrong policy guidanceA requestor is told something is eligible, builds a programme around it, and is declined.
Guidance is retrieved from the company's own documents and quoted with clause and version. Eligibility is a rulebook result, not an opinion. Questions outside the corpus are declined and routed. Every answer is logged with its sources for review.

    Bad data in the system of recordDuplicate organizations, wrong grant type, invented figures.
Search-before-create; type confirmed with the requestor before the schema is fixed; every value has a source and a confirmation; writes only at checkpoints, idempotent, through the platform's validation; sandbox certification per company.

    Rulebook drifts from the platformRequests pass the agent and fail on submit, or the agent asks for things the platform no longer needs.
Import, not retype; nightly comparison; test set per subtype and country run against the sandbox; a platform validation error at submit is a logged defect with an owner.

    The agent becomes an advocateRequestors use it to craft answers that get to yes, or it fills in attestations and rationale for them.
Hard scope boundaries in the orchestrator; attestations and conflict-of-interest answers typed by the requestor; the educational rationale is extracted from their documents, never drafted; the review summary tells the coordinator exactly what the agent did.

    Review after submission gets weakerCoordinators trust the summary and skip the judgement.
The summary lists checks and evidence, not conclusions; the platform checklist still runs; the items that need judgement are labelled "for you"; the vendor's coordinator-side agent reads the same rulebook and shows the same evidence.

    Privacy and sensitive dataBank details, signer identities and board members pass through a chat.
Bank and payee details never enter the conversation; they stay in the portal's secure flow. Personal data is minimised to what the platform already holds. Per-company model hosting and residency; no training on requestor data.

    Integration effort dominatesEvery company's org is configured differently.
Metadata read from the org; mapping configured, not coded; the end-to-end test on screen 3 is the acceptance test; integration priced as a separate line.

  For our next conversationOpen questions

    Where the agent lives. Embedded in the requestor portal under the portal login, or a standalone branded page beside it? Embedded gives one identity and one audit trail; standalone is easier to sell across companies and platforms. I'd build the service once and embed first.

    Identity for writes. Can the connector act as the requestor through the portal's login, or will the company insist on a service account? The answer shapes audit, emails and the "created by" on every request.

    Who owns the rulebook. The company's grants process owner, the vendor's grants practice, or both with a two-signature publish? Also: will the vendor's coordinator-side review agent read it, so the before and after reviews agree?

    Scope of the first pilot. Education grants only, or education plus charitable contributions? The second adds the organization-screening rules and the "no business benefit" test, which are the hard cases for a chat agent.

    Follow-ups in scope. Submission only, or also additional-information rounds, reconciliation and outcomes? The follow-ups are where requestors struggle most today and where the coordinator time goes.

    Model and hosting. Does each company choose model and region, or does the vendor host one service? Data residency for European requestors and the company's AI governance review decide this.

    Attestation wording. Legal review of how the requestor's statements are presented and recorded when an agent prepared the request. Cheap to get right early; expensive to change.

    Internal organizers. Should coordinators who create requests internally (memberships, product donations) use the same agent in the first release?

    Languages. Which of the portal's seven languages matter for the pilot, and does the policy corpus exist in them?

    Measuring success. Baseline today for incomplete submissions, information rounds per request and days to a complete submission, so the pilot can show a change.
